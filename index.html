§1 · Fractals

The Mandelbrot Set

Benoît Mandelbrot · IBM · 1980

z ↦ z2 + c

Pick a point c on the plane. Start with z = 0, then keep replacing z with z² + c. For some points the numbers stay small forever. Those points make up the Mandelbrot set, shown here in black.

For every other point, the numbers eventually rush off toward infinity. The color shows how many steps that took. The edge between the two is infinitely detailed: zoom in anywhere along it and you find spirals, seahorses, lightning bolts and tiny copies of the whole set. Nobody designed any of it. It all comes from one line of algebra.

Try this

  • Tap or click anywhere on the plate to zoom in there. Drag with a mouse to move around.
  • With a mouse, hover over a point to see its orbit, the path z takes as the rule repeats. Inside the set the orbit circles forever. Outside, it escapes.
  • Press A tiny copy to find a miniature Mandelbrot set hiding on the needle to the left.
The math behind it

Why stop at 2? Once |z| is bigger than 2, squaring it always makes it grow faster than adding c can pull it back, so it is certain to escape. The program can stop counting there.

Smooth colors. Counting whole steps gives hard color bands. The shader uses a fractional count, n + 1 − log₂(ln |z|), which blends one band into the next.

Why the zoom stops. Graphics cards use 32-bit numbers with about 7 significant digits. Past roughly 25,000× zoom, neighboring pixels round to the same number and the picture turns to blocks. Deep-zoom videos use special software with hundreds of digits.

§2 · Waves

Drawing with Circles

Joseph Fourier · Paris · 1807

f(t) = Σ cn e2πint

Fourier made a claim that sounded absurd: any repeating pattern, however jagged, can be built by adding up simple waves. Seen from above, a wave is just a circle turning at a steady speed.

Chain circles together, each one riding on the rim of the one before, and the tip of the last circle can trace any closed shape. A close cousin of this idea compresses the music on your phone (MP3) and the photos you send (JPEG), and lets MRI scanners turn radio signals into pictures of the inside of your body.

Try this

  • Slide the number of circles down to 3. You get a wobbly blob. Slide it back up and the shape snaps into focus.
  • Pick Star or Square. Sharp corners need far more circles than smooth curves.
  • Press Draw your own and sketch any single loop, such as the first letter of your name.
The math behind it

The outline is sampled at 256 evenly spaced points, each treated as a complex number x + iy. A discrete Fourier transform turns those 256 points into 255 circles, each with a size, a starting angle and a whole-number speed n.

The circles are sorted largest first. That is why the first handful do most of the work and the rest only add fine detail.

§3 · Chaos

The Butterfly Effect

Edward Lorenz · MIT · 1963

dxdt = σ(y − x) dydt = x(ρ − z) − y dzdt = xy − βz

In 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz reran a weather simulation. To save time he typed in a number from a printout, 0.506, instead of the 0.506127 stored in the computer. That tiny difference grew until the forecast was completely different.

He boiled the problem down to these three equations. On the plate, two paths start one millionth of a unit apart. For a while they move as one. Then they split and never agree again, though both stay on the same butterfly-shaped track, now called the Lorenz attractor. This is why detailed weather forecasts lose their accuracy after a week or two.

Try this

  • Watch the chart. On a log scale the gap climbs in a straight line, which is the signature of exponential growth.
  • Switch the starting gap to 10⁻⁹, a thousand times smaller. The paths stay together longer, but only by about 7 time units. Better measurements buy surprisingly little.
  • Drag the plate sideways to turn the attractor in 3D.
The math behind it

These are Lorenz's original values: σ = 10, ρ = 28, β = 8⁄3. The equations are solved with the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method, 250 small steps per unit of time.

The gap grows roughly like e0.9t. The number 0.906 is the system's largest Lyapunov exponent. Shrinking the starting error 1,000 times delays the split by only ln(1000) ⁄ 0.906 ≈ 7.6 time units.

§4 · Nature

The Sunflower's Secret

Helmut Vogel's model · 1979

θ = 360° ⁄ φ2 ≈ 137.508°

A sunflower grows its seeds from the center outward, each new seed at a fixed turn from the one before. Which angle packs the most seeds with no wasted space?

If the turn is a simple fraction of a circle, like 2⁄5, the seeds line up in straight spokes with gaps between them. The best angle is the one furthest from every simple fraction. That is the golden angle, 137.508°, built from the golden ratio φ = (1 + √5) ⁄ 2. Count the spirals it makes and you find neighboring Fibonacci numbers such as 21, 34 and 55, the same numbers you can count on real sunflowers, pinecones and pineapples.

Try this

  • Drag the angle a fraction of a degree away from 137.508°. The perfect packing breaks into spokes.
  • Press 2⁄5 turn for 5 straight arms, or 8⁄21 turn for 21.
  • Press Sweep to glide slowly through nearby angles.
The math behind it

Seed n sits at angle n × θ and distance c√n from the center. The square root gives every seed the same area.

φ is called the "most irrational" number because its continued fraction is all ones: 1 + 1 ⁄ (1 + 1 ⁄ (1 + …)). Fractions approximate it worse than any other number, so the seeds never line up. Its best approximations are ratios of Fibonacci numbers, 13⁄8, 21⁄13, 34⁄21, and those are the spiral counts you see.

§5 · Number theory

Spirals of Primes

Explained by Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown) · 2019

(r, θ) = (p, p)

Prime numbers have no divisors except 1 and themselves: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 and so on. They seem to turn up at random. Here is an odd way to plot them: put each whole number n at distance n from the center, turned n radians around, then keep only the primes.

Zoom out and spirals appear. Zoom out further and they straighten into rays. The cause is how well fractions approximate 2π. Because 44⁄7 is close to 2π, numbers 44 apart land almost in line. Because 710⁄113 is even closer, numbers 710 apart form nearly straight rays. Some spirals stay empty because their numbers all share a factor with 44 or 710, so they can't be prime. That leaves primes on 20 of the 44 spirals and 280 of the 710 rays.

Try this

  • Press Zoom out and watch. Around 3,000 you can count the spirals. Keep going past 50,000 for the rays.
  • Switch to All numbers. The spirals come from the plotting method itself; primes only decide which ones are empty.
  • Slide all the way in to read the first primes by name.
The math behind it

44 radians is 7 full turns plus 0.0177 radians. So n and n + 44 sit at almost the same angle, and each group of numbers with the same remainder when divided by 44 forms a slowly curving spiral. Numbers with an even remainder, or a remainder of 11 or 33, are divisible by 2 or 11. That rules out 24 of the 44 spirals.

710 radians is 113 turns plus only 0.00006 radians, so those groups curve so slowly they look straight. 710 = 2 × 5 × 71, which leaves 280 remainders that primes can use.

The primes are found with the Sieve of Eratosthenes: all 25,997 primes below 300,000 in a few milliseconds.

§6 · Probability

Finding π by Chance

Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon · 1777

π ≈ 2N ⁄ C

Drop a needle on a floor of wooden planks, where the needle is exactly as long as a plank is wide. What is the chance it lands across a crack? Buffon worked out the answer: 2⁄π.

So π is hiding in a game of chance. Drop N needles, count the C that cross a line, and 2N ÷ C creeps toward 3.14159. It gets there slowly: each extra correct digit takes roughly 100 times as many needles. In 1901 the Italian mathematician Mario Lazzarini claimed 3.1415929 from just 3,408 needles, correct to six decimal places. That is so unlikely that most historians think he stopped when the answer looked good.

Try this

  • Drop one needle at a time and see how wild the early guesses are.
  • Press Rain and watch the estimate settle onto the π line in the chart.
  • Watch the yellow digits. They show how much of your estimate is right.
The math behind it

A needle of length ℓ at angle θ crosses a line when its center lies within (ℓ⁄2) sin θ of one. Averaging sin θ over every possible angle gives 2⁄π, so the chance of a crossing is 2ℓ ⁄ (πd). With ℓ = d, that is 2⁄π.

Using randomness to calculate a number is called a Monte Carlo method. Scientists use it to simulate everything from nuclear reactors to the spread of diseases.

How it was made

This site is nearly 4,000 lines of hand-written HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no frameworks, libraries, images or videos. Every picture is computed on your device.

Each experiment is built on a different technique from computer science.

§1 · GPU programmingWebGL fragment shader
A small program that runs on the graphics card, once for every pixel, all in parallel.
§2 · Signal processingDiscrete Fourier transform
Turns 256 points of a drawing into 255 rotating circles.
§3 · Numerical methodsRunge–Kutta integration
Steps three differential equations forward 250 times per unit of time.
§4 · Computational geometryNeighbor search
Finds which seeds touch, then groups them into spiral families.
§5 · AlgorithmsSieve of Eratosthenes
Crosses out multiples to find every prime below 300,000.
§6 · StatisticsMonte Carlo simulation
Uses thousands of random trials to estimate a number.
