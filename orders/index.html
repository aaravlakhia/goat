1026 m10 to the power 26 meters 1.3 billion light-years away

A ripple is on its way

1.3 billion years ago, in a galaxy far from ours, two black holes spiraled into each other and merged. The crash shook space itself and sent a ripple outward at the speed of light.

When it set off, there were no animals or plants on Earth. Keep this ripple in mind. You will meet it again at the very end, at the smallest size of all.

1021 m10 to the power 21 meters our galaxy

The Milky Way

Hundreds of billions of stars in a spiral about 100,000 light-years across. The Sun is the star marked “You are here”, about 26,000 light-years from the center.

In 1610 Galileo pointed one of the first telescopes at the pale band across the night sky and saw that it was made of countless stars.

1017 m10 to the power 17 meters about ten light-years

The nearest stars

A light-year is how far light travels in a year: about 9.5 trillion kilometers. The closest star, Proxima Centauri, is 4.2 light-years away, so we see it as it was 4.2 years ago.

Nothing can travel faster than light. Einstein explained why in 1905.

1013 m10 to the power 13 meters the Solar System

The Sun’s family

Eight planets, all following Newton’s law of gravity: the inner ones race around while the outer ones crawl. The long ellipse is Halley’s Comet, which comes back about every 76 years.

In 1846 astronomers used Newton’s law to work out where an unseen planet must be tugging on Uranus. They pointed a telescope there and found Neptune.

109 m10 to the power 9 meters the Moon’s orbit

1687 Isaac Newton

The Moon is falling

Why does an apple fall while the Moon stays up? Newton’s answer: the Moon does not stay up. The same gravity pulls on both.

Every second the Moon falls about 1.4 mm toward Earth, but it also moves about 1 km sideways, so it never gets any closer. It falls forever and keeps missing. That is what an orbit is.

F = G m₁m₂ / r² gravity weakens with the square of the distance

Built on Galileo, 1638 · Led to Einstein, 1905

108 m10 to the power 8 meters GPS satellites

1905 Albert Einstein

Time runs at different speeds

Einstein showed that a clock moving fast ticks more slowly, and later that a clock in weaker gravity ticks faster. GPS satellites feel both.

Racing along at 14,000 km/h, their clocks lose 7 millionths of a second a day. Twenty thousand kilometers up, where gravity is weaker, they gain 45. Your phone corrects for the difference of 38. Without that, GPS would drift by about 10 km every day.

t′ = t / √(1 − v²/c²) a moving clock runs slow

Built on Maxwell, 1865 and Newton, 1687 · Led to Meitner, 1938 and LIGO, 2015

102 m10 to the power 2 meters the Leaning Tower of Pisa

1638 Galileo Galilei

Heavy things don’t fall faster

For almost 2,000 years people believed Aristotle: a heavy stone falls faster than a light one. Galileo tested it. Apart from the push of the air, everything falls the same way, speeding up at the same rate.

Nobody knows if he really dropped balls from this tower. His real experiments timed balls rolling down ramps. In 1971 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the airless Moon, and they landed together.

d = ½ g t² the distance fallen grows with the square of the time

Led to Newton, 1687

10−1 m10 to the power minus 1 meters a coil and a magnet

1831 Michael Faraday

Moving magnets make electricity

Push a magnet into a coil of wire and an electric current flows. Hold it still and the current stops. Pull it out and the current flows the other way.

Faraday, who left school at 13, discovered this in 1831. Nearly every power station on Earth still makes electricity his way, by spinning magnets past coils of wire.

ε = −dΦ/dt a changing magnetic field drives a current

Led to Maxwell, 1865

10−2 m10 to the power minus 2 meters a speck of radium

1898 Marie Skłodowska-Curie

Atoms are not forever

Some atoms break apart by themselves, shooting out particles and energy. Marie Skłodowska-Curie called this radioactivity, and in 1898 she and Pierre Curie discovered two new elements by their rays: polonium and radium.

This is a cloud chamber. Each alpha particle from the radium leaves a trail of mist a few centimeters long, then stops. Her notebooks are still radioactive today.

²²⁶Ra → ²²²Rn + α radium breaks down into radon and an alpha particle

Led to Rutherford, 1911

10−4 m10 to the power minus 4 meters a human hair

A human hair

About 70 micrometers (0.07 mm) across, and covered in overlapping scales. The red discs are blood cells, each about 7 micrometers wide.

From here on, everything is too small to see with your eyes alone.

10−6 m10 to the power minus 6 meters waves of light

1865 James Clerk Maxwell

Light is a wave of electricity and magnetism

Maxwell wrote four equations that join electricity and magnetism together. They predicted waves that travel at about 300,000 km every second, the speed already measured for light. So light had to be one of these waves.

Each color is a different wavelength, drawn here to scale: about 700 nanometers for red, down to about 420 for violet. Longer waves are infrared, microwaves and radio. Shorter ones are ultraviolet and X-rays.

c = 1 / √(μ₀ε₀) the speed of light, from electricity and magnetism

Built on Faraday, 1831 · Led to Einstein, 1905

10−8 m10 to the power minus 8 meters molecules of benzene

1928 C. V. Raman

Light comes back a new color

When light bounces off molecules, about one photon in ten million comes back a slightly different color. It has given a little energy to the molecule’s vibrations, or taken some. Each kind of molecule shifts the color by its own amounts, like a fingerprint.

Raman found this in Calcutta in 1928 using sunlight, a telescope lens and colored filters, and won India’s first Nobel Prize in science. Raman scanners now spot fake medicines without opening the packet.

Eout = Ein − Evibration the energy lost is the molecule’s fingerprint

Built on Einstein, 1905

10−9 m10 to the power minus 9 meters a hydrogen atom

1913 Niels Bohr

Electrons live on fixed levels

Heat any element and it glows with its own set of exact colors, like a barcode. Bohr explained why. The electron in an atom can only be on certain levels, and when it drops to a lower one it gives out light of one exact color.

Hydrogen’s red glow comes from the drop from level 3 to level 2. Barcodes like this tell us what the stars are made of.

En = −13.6 eV / n² the energy of level n in hydrogen

Built on Rutherford, 1911 · Led to de Broglie, 1924

10−9 m10 to the power minus 9 meters the same atom, as waves

1924 Louis de Broglie

Matter is a wave

Why only those levels? De Broglie suggested that every particle is also a wave. An electron’s wave has to fit around its orbit a whole number of times, or it cancels itself out. One wave fits level 1, two fit level 2, three fit level 3.

In 1927, electrons fired at crystals made the stripes that only waves can make. Electron microscopes use these waves today.

λ = h / p a particle’s wavelength, from its momentum

Built on Bohr, 1913 and Einstein, 1905

10−13 m10 to the power minus 13 meters inside a gold atom

1911 Ernest Rutherford

The atom is almost empty

Since the last stop you have zoomed more than 1,000 times smaller, through nothing but empty space. Rutherford’s students Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, who was 20, fired alpha particles at gold foil. Nearly all went straight through, but about 1 in 8,000 bounced back.

Rutherford said: “It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.” Nearly all of an atom’s mass is in a nucleus about 100,000 times smaller than the atom.

atom ≈ 100,000 × nucleus almost all of an atom is empty space

Built on Curie, 1898 · Led to Bohr, 1913 and Meitner, 1938

10−14 m10 to the power minus 14 meters a uranium nucleus

1938 Lise Meitner

The nucleus can split

In 1938 Otto Hahn found barium in uranium that had been hit by neutrons, and could not explain it. Lise Meitner, who had fled Nazi Germany, worked it out on a walk in the snow with her nephew Otto Frisch: the nucleus had split in two.

The pieces weigh a little less than the whole, and the missing mass becomes energy. Each split also frees neutrons that can split more nuclei: a chain reaction. The Nobel Prize went to Hahn alone.

E = mc² a little mass becomes a lot of energy

Built on Einstein, 1905 and Rutherford, 1911

10−15 m10 to the power minus 15 meters a proton

Inside a proton

Every nucleus is made of protons and neutrons, and each of those is made of three quarks held together by gluons. A proton is about 1.7 × 10−15 m across.

Surely nothing smaller could ever be measured. Keep zooming.

10−17 m10 to the power minus 17 meters LIGO’s measurement

2015 Weiss, Thorne & Barish (LIGO)

Space itself can ripple

Remember the ripple from the start? On 14 September 2015 it reached Earth. It stretched one 4 km arm of each LIGO detector and squeezed the other, changing their lengths by about 4 × 10−18 m: around 1/400 of the width of a proton. LIGO’s lasers caught it.

It was the first gravitational wave ever detected, 99 years after Einstein predicted them. The trace on screen has the shape of the signal: the black holes’ last orbits, faster and faster, then the crash.

h = ΔL / L ≈ 10−21 how much space was stretched

Built on Einstein, 1905

    Using it in a lesson

    Put it on a projector and press Play the journey. It zooms by itself and stops at each discovery; press Space or ↓ to move on early, and ↑ to go back. On a phone, just scroll.

    1. The Moon and LIGO’s mirrors are both moved by gravity. Why was one easy to measure in 1687 and the other only possible in 2015?
    2. Galileo, Newton and Einstein all appear near the size of the Earth. Why do you think the earliest discoveries were made at human size and bigger?
    3. Rutherford found the atom is almost empty. So why can’t your hand pass through a table?
