1638

Galileo Galilei

Lived
1564–1642
Where
Pisa, Padua and Florence, Italy
Age
74, when he published
Book
Two New Sciences

d ∝ t2

Heavy things don't fall faster.

For almost 2,000 years, people believed Aristotle: a heavy stone falls faster than a light one. Galileo Galilei decided to test it.

The famous story of him dropping balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa probably never happened. His real experiments rolled balls down gentle ramps, timed with a water clock. He found that everything speeds up in exactly the same way. Only air makes a feather lag behind. The distance fallen grows with the square of the time: in equal moments, a falling object covers 1, then 3, then 5, then 7 units.

He published this in 1638, at the age of 74, while under house arrest for teaching that the Earth moves around the Sun.

What it set off

  1. 1656Christiaan Huygens builds the first pendulum clock, using Galileo's discovery that a pendulum keeps steady time.
  2. 1687Isaac Newton turns Galileo's falling objects into three laws of motion.
  3. 1971On the Moon, astronaut David Scott drops a hammer and a feather. They land together.
  4. 2022The MICROSCOPE satellite confirms Galileo's rule to one part in a thousand trillion.

1687

Isaac Newton

Lived
1643–1727
Where
Cambridge, England
Age
44
Book
Principia

F = Gm1m2 ⁄ r2

The Moon is falling, and always missing.

Newton said that watching an apple fall made him wonder how far the Earth's pull could reach. His answer: all the way to the Moon.

He imagined a cannon on a very tall mountain. Fire it gently and the ball lands nearby. Fire it harder and it lands further away. Fire it fast enough and the ground curves away beneath the ball as quickly as it falls, so it keeps falling forever and never lands. That is an orbit. The Moon does exactly this, pulled by the same gravity that pulls an apple.

His book Principia (1687) explained apples, tides, planets and comets with a single law. His friend Edmond Halley paid for it to be printed.

If I have seen further it is by standing on the shoulders of Giants.

Isaac Newton, in a letter to Robert Hooke, 1675

What it set off

  1. 1758Halley's comet returns exactly when Newton's laws said it would, 16 years after Halley's death.
  2. 1846Astronomers find Neptune where the math predicted, before anyone had seen it.
  3. 1957Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite, circles the Earth at about 8 km/s.
  4. 1969Apollo 11 lands on the Moon, steered the whole way by Newton's laws.

1831

Michael Faraday

Lived
1791–1867
Where
Royal Institution, London
Age
39
School
Left at 13

ℰ = −dΦ ⁄ dt

Move a magnet, make electricity.

Michael Faraday grew up poor and left school at 13 to work for a bookbinder. He read the science books he was binding, went to public lectures by the chemist Humphry Davy, and sent Davy a neatly bound copy of his notes. Davy gave him a job as his lab assistant.

In 1831 Faraday pushed a magnet into a coil of wire, and the needle on his meter flicked. When he held the magnet still, nothing happened. When he pulled it out, the needle flicked the other way. Electricity comes from change: a moving magnet, or a changing magnetic field.

Faraday knew very little mathematics. He pictured invisible "lines of force" around magnets instead. A young Scottish physicist would later turn those pictures into equations.

What it set off

  1. 1832Hippolyte Pixii builds the first electric generator by spinning a magnet past coils.
  2. 1882Edison's Pearl Street Station starts selling electricity to homes and offices in New York.
  3. 1896Generators at Niagara Falls send power 40 km to the city of Buffalo.
  4. TodayCoal, gas, nuclear, wind and hydro plants all make electricity by spinning magnets past coils. Your phone's wireless charger uses the same idea.

1865

James Clerk Maxwell

Lived
1831–1879
Where
King's College, London
Age
34
Born
The year Faraday found induction

c = 1 ⁄ √(μ0ε0)

Light is electricity and magnetism.

James Clerk Maxwell took Faraday's pictures of force lines and wrote them as equations. The equations predicted something nobody had imagined: waves of electric and magnetic field that travel on their own, even through empty space.

Maxwell worked out how fast these waves should travel, using only lab measurements of magnets and electric charges. The answer was almost exactly the speed of light. Light, he realized, is one of these waves. So are many kinds of invisible light that nobody had detected yet.

We can scarcely avoid the inference that light consists in the transverse undulations of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena.

James Clerk Maxwell, 1862

What it set off

  1. 1887Heinrich Hertz makes and detects radio waves in his lab, proving Maxwell right.
  2. 1895Jagadish Chandra Bose uses microwaves in Kolkata to ring a bell at a distance.
  3. 1901Guglielmo Marconi sends a radio signal across the Atlantic Ocean.
  4. TodayRadio, TV, Wi-Fi, mobile networks, GPS, radar, X-ray scans and microwave ovens all use Maxwell's waves.

1898

Marie Skłodowska-Curie

Lived
1867–1934
Where
Paris, France (born in Warsaw, Poland)
Age
31
Nobels
Physics 1903, Chemistry 1911

N = N0 · 2−t ⁄ T

Atoms are not forever.

In 1896 Henri Becquerel found that uranium gives off invisible rays. Marie Skłodowska-Curie set out to discover why. She showed the rays come from inside the atoms themselves, and named the effect radioactivity.

With her husband Pierre, she ground up tonnes of a uranium ore called pitchblende and in 1898 found two new elements: polonium, named after her homeland, Poland, and radium, which glows in the dark. Radioactive atoms break apart into other elements. Nobody can say when any single atom will go, but in a large group, half of them decay in a fixed time called the half-life.

She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, and she is still the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

What it set off

  1. 1903Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel.
  2. 1914In World War I, Curie drives X-ray vans to the front line so surgeons can find bullets and shrapnel.
  3. 1949Willard Libby uses the steady decay of carbon-14 to date ancient objects: radiocarbon dating.
  4. TodayAbout half of all cancer patients are treated with radiation therapy.

The cost

Curie often carried test tubes of radium in her pockets. She died in 1934 of a blood disease almost certainly caused by radiation. Her notebooks are still radioactive and are kept in lead-lined boxes in Paris. Anyone who wants to read them must sign a waiver.

1905

Albert Einstein

Lived
1879–1955
Where
Patent office, Bern, Switzerland
Age
26
Nobel
1921, for a different 1905 paper

t′ = t ⁄ √(1 − v2⁄c2)

Moving clocks run slow.

In 1905 Albert Einstein was a 26-year-old clerk at the patent office in Bern. In his spare time that year he wrote four papers that changed physics.

One started from a simple rule: light always travels at the same speed, however fast you are moving. If that is true, then time itself has to stretch. A clock moving past you ticks slower than your own. The effect is tiny at everyday speeds and enormous close to the speed of light. Another paper from the same year gave the most famous equation in physics, E = mc²: mass is a huge store of energy.

His Nobel Prize wasn't for relativity. It was for a third 1905 paper, which showed that light arrives in particles.

What it set off

  1. 1919Arthur Eddington photographs a solar eclipse and finds starlight bent by the Sun's gravity, as Einstein predicted.
  2. 1971Physicists fly atomic clocks around the world on passenger jets. The clocks come back out of step, exactly as relativity says.
  3. 1978The first GPS satellite is launched, carrying atomic clocks.
  4. TodayGPS satellite clocks gain 38 microseconds a day. Without Einstein's correction, your phone's map would drift by about 10 km a day.

1911

Ernest Rutherford

Lived
1871–1937
Where
Manchester, England (born in New Zealand)
Age
39
Nobel
Chemistry 1908, three years earlier

N(θ) ∝ 1 ⁄ sin4(θ⁄2)

The atom is almost empty.

In 1909 Ernest Rutherford asked Hans Geiger and a 20-year-old student, Ernest Marsden, to fire alpha particles at a thin sheet of gold foil. Sitting in the dark, they counted tiny flashes where the particles hit a screen.

At the time, most physicists pictured an atom as a soft ball of positive charge with electrons dotted through it, like a plum pudding. Alpha particles should have passed straight through. Almost all of them did. But about 1 in 8,000 bounced back.

It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.

Ernest Rutherford

In 1911 Rutherford explained it. Nearly all of an atom's mass sits in a tiny, dense nucleus, about 100,000 times smaller than the atom itself. The rest is empty space, and that includes every atom in you.

What it set off

  1. 1917Rutherford turns nitrogen into oxygen by hitting it with alpha particles: the first nuclear reaction made by people.
  2. 1932James Chadwick, in Rutherford's lab, discovers the neutron, the particle that would later split uranium.
  3. 1932John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton, also in his lab, split atoms with the first particle accelerator.
  4. 2012CERN's 27 km Large Hadron Collider, a descendant of this experiment, discovers the Higgs boson.

1913

Niels Bohr

Lived
1885–1962
Where
Copenhagen, Denmark
Age
27
Nobel
Physics 1922

En = −13.6 eV ⁄ n2

Electrons jump.

Heat hydrogen gas and it glows. Pass that glow through a prism and you don't get a rainbow. You get four thin lines of color: red, cyan, blue and violet. Nobody knew why.

Niels Bohr, who had just worked with Rutherford in Manchester, proposed a strange rule. An electron in an atom can only sit on certain energy levels, never in between. When it drops from a higher level to a lower one, it gives off one packet of light whose color exactly matches the size of the drop. Different drops, different colors. His formula predicted hydrogen's lines perfectly.

It meant every element has its own barcode of colors. That is how we know what the Sun and distant stars are made of.

What it set off

  1. 1925Werner Heisenberg and Erwin Schrödinger turn Bohr's jumping electrons into quantum mechanics.
  2. 1960Theodore Maiman builds the first laser, which makes huge numbers of electrons drop together.
  3. 1990sBlue LEDs are invented, making bright white LED lighting possible.
  4. 2022The James Webb Space Telescope reads the light of a planet 700 light-years away and finds carbon dioxide in its air.

Strange but true

In 1940, as Nazi troops entered Copenhagen, the chemist George de Hevesy dissolved two colleagues' gold Nobel medals in acid at Bohr's institute so they couldn't be seized. The jar sat on a shelf through the war. Afterwards the gold was recovered and the medals were recast.

1924

Louis de Broglie

Lived
1892–1987
Where
Paris, France
Age
32
First degree
History

λ = h ⁄ p

Matter is a wave.

Einstein had shown that light, a wave, can behave like a stream of particles. In his 1924 PhD thesis, Louis de Broglie asked the reverse: can particles, such as electrons, behave like waves?

His examiners weren't sure what to make of it, so they sent the thesis to Einstein, who approved. Three years later, experiments showed that electrons really do spread out and interfere like waves. Fire electrons one at a time at two narrow slits and each one lands as a single dot. Yet thousands of dots build up a pattern of stripes, as if each electron went through both slits at once.

Try to watch which slit each electron goes through, and the stripes disappear.

A phenomenon which is impossible, absolutely impossible, to explain in any classical way, and which has in it the heart of quantum mechanics.

Richard Feynman, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, 1965

What it set off

  1. 1927Experiments show electrons acting as waves. One was by George Thomson, whose father J. J. Thomson had won a Nobel for showing the electron is a particle.
  2. 1931Ernst Ruska and Max Knoll build the first electron microscope. It would soon show viruses for the first time.
  3. 1947The transistor is invented at Bell Labs, built on the quantum behavior of electrons in crystals.
  4. TodayA phone's processor holds around 20 billion transistors, each far smaller than a virus.

1928

C. V. Raman

Lived
1888–1970
Where
Calcutta, India
Age
39
Nobel
Physics 1930

νout = νin − νmolecule

Light can change color.

In 1921, sailing home to India from Europe, C. V. Raman stared at the deep blue of the Mediterranean Sea. The accepted explanation was that the sea simply reflects the blue sky. Raman didn't believe it. He tested it on deck with a small prism and became convinced the water itself was scattering the light.

Back in Calcutta, he and his student K. S. Krishnan shone strong, filtered sunlight through dozens of liquids. On 28 February 1928 they saw it: a tiny fraction of the scattered light comes out a slightly different color. The shift comes from the vibrations of the molecules, so every substance has its own pattern of shifted colors, like a fingerprint.

India celebrates 28 February as National Science Day in his honor.

What it set off

  1. 1930Raman wins the Nobel Prize in Physics, the first Asian to win a Nobel in science.
  2. 1960sLasers make Raman's faint effect easy to measure, turning it into a tool for identifying substances.
  3. 2000sHandheld Raman scanners check medicines for fakes and luggage for explosives, and help experts identify the paints in old artworks.
  4. 2021NASA's Perseverance rover lands on Mars carrying SHERLOC, a Raman instrument that searches rocks for signs of ancient life.

Also from India, the same decade

In 1924 Satyendra Nath Bose sent Einstein a paper, which Einstein translated into German and had published. Particles called bosons, including the Higgs boson, are named after Bose.

In 1930, on a ship to England, 19-year-old Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, Raman's nephew, calculated the heaviest a white dwarf star can be before it collapses. It won him a Nobel Prize in 1983.

And Raman was born on 7 November, the same birthday as Marie Curie.

Lise Meitner

Lived
1878–1968
Where

Age

The nucleus splits.

Lise Meitner spent 30 years in Berlin studying radioactivity with the chemist Otto Hahn. She was Jewish, and in July 1938 she escaped Nazi Germany with a small suitcase and a diamond ring Hahn gave her in case she needed money.

That December, Hahn wrote to her with a puzzle: firing neutrons at uranium had produced barium, an element about half its size. Over Christmas, walking in the snow in Sweden with her nephew Otto Frisch, Meitner worked it out. The uranium nucleus wobbles like a drop of water and splits in two. Using E = mc², they calculated the huge energy each split releases. Frisch named it fission.

Within weeks, other physicists found that each split also frees two or three neutrons. Those can split more nuclei, which free more neutrons. A chain reaction.

I will have nothing to do with a bomb!

Lise Meitner, refusing to join the project to build the atomic bomb

What it set off

  1. 1942Enrico Fermi's team runs the first controlled chain reaction, in a squash court under a football stadium in Chicago.
  2. 1945Atomic bombs destroy Hiroshima and Nagasaki, killing well over 100,000 people.
  3. 1954The first nuclear power station to supply an electricity grid opens in Obninsk, near Moscow.
  4. TodayNuclear power stations make about a tenth of the world's electricity, with almost no carbon emissions.

The cost

The same discovery made the most destructive weapons ever built, and about 12,000 nuclear warheads exist today. Meitner refused to work on the bomb. The 1944 Nobel Prize for the discovery went to Hahn alone. In 1997, element 109 was named meitnerium in her honor.

2015

Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne & Barry Barish

Where
LIGO: Hanford, Washington and Livingston, Louisiana, USA
Team
More than 1,000 scientists
Predicted
By Einstein, 99 years earlier
Nobel
Physics 2017

h = ΔL ⁄ L ≈ 10−21

Space itself shakes.

In 1916 Einstein predicted that colliding masses should send ripples through space itself, stretching and squeezing everything they pass. He also thought the ripples would be far too small ever to measure.

In 1972 Rainer Weiss sketched a way to do it: a giant L-shaped instrument with laser beams running along two 4 km arms. A passing wave would stretch one arm and squeeze the other by less than a ten-thousandth of the width of a proton. Building it took more than 40 years and over a thousand people.

On 14 September 2015, both LIGO detectors, 3,000 km apart, caught the same 0.2-second signal, 7 milliseconds apart. Two black holes, 36 and 29 times the mass of the Sun, had spiraled together 1.3 billion years ago. In the final fraction of a second, they turned three Suns' worth of mass into gravitational waves.

What it set off

  1. 2016The discovery is announced, and the sound of the collision is played around the world.
  2. 2017Detectors catch two neutron stars colliding, and telescopes see the explosion that forged heavy elements such as gold.
  3. TodayGravitational-wave detectors have recorded hundreds of black hole collisions.
  4. 2035LISA, a detector made of three spacecraft 2.5 million km apart, is planned to launch.

How it was made

Hand-written HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no libraries, images or videos. Each experiment is a small physics simulation that runs on your device: gravity, induction, electromagnetic waves, random decay, relativity, Coulomb scattering, energy levels, quantum probability, light scattering, neutron chain reactions and a gravitational-wave chirp.

The universe behind the page is written directly in WebGL2: one shader turns each particle's random numbers into sixteen formations, and the graphics card records every particle's position so they can fly smoothly from one scene to the next.

Experiments pause when they scroll out of view, and none start moving on their own if your device is set to reduce motion. The pause button in the top bar stops the background too.

Made to be read by everyone: the Display button sets text size, contrast, an easy-to-read typeface, spacing, motion and how bright the universe is, and every chapter can be read aloud. Everything works with a keyboard and a screen reader.
